5.Discussion
MaricultureintheU.S.hassignificantpotentialfor
successnowandinthefuture,likelybenefitingfrom
awarmingocean.Ourresults,thefirstusingfineres-
olutionstatisticallydownscaledmodeloutputsforthe
U.S.,broadlymatchcoarser,global-scaleassessments
ofclimatechangeimpactsonmariculture[ 14,15,44].
However,weprovidedetailedinsightatthefinerspa-
tial, taxonomic, and policy resolution necessary to
anticipate and plan for climate change. Overall, we
findincreasesintemperatureprimarilydrivechanges
in performance—as northern latitudes get warmer,
performanceimproves,whiletemperatureincreases
in southern latitudes exceed the thermal ranges of
many species. Therefore, policy could be updated
within each state to implement a formal review of
permitted aquaculture species regularly to determ-
ine if other species should be included or removed
basedonperformanceincurrentwatertemperatures.
Additionally,manystatesdonotcurrentlypermitthe
mariculture of any finfish or seaweed species des-
pitetheircurrentandfutureperformancepotential.
Inclusionofthesetaxontothemaricultureportfolio
coulddiversifyseafoodproductionandincreasesta-
bilityinthesector.
WhileoverallmaricultureperformanceintheU.S.
increases in the future, climate change will amp-
lify existing regional differences, with larger effects
in the less optimistic climate scenarios and over
longer time domains. For example, climate ‘win-
ners’ include wealthy states (e.g. California, New
York)whileclimate‘losers’includepoorerstates(e.g.
Mississippi,Louisiana).Ourfindingsalignwithother
climatechangeimpacts tofood systems,painting a
bleak picture for the south. For example, climate
changeimpactstofreshwateraquaculturewilllikely

are projected to have the greatest decrease in pro-

wheat, soy, and cotton as the environment warms,
